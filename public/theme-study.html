Version 15 / Living lines and longer journeys

Identity need not imply the same contour, speed, or ending.

The earlier generator imposed a register crest, a late quotation and a homeward ending on too many arguments. Those rules made very different intentions sound alike. This revision gives the source itself several contour families, then develops it through related clauses. An ascending line can hand off to a new section; a descending line can settle without first climbing to an obligatory high point.

Solo as developing speech

Guthrie Govan describes musical vocabulary as a basis for improvisation, values varied material, and distinguishes improvising within established songs from reproducing an earlier solo. Our design inference: retain a small recognizable subject, vary the length and rhythm of its clauses, and combine sequence, inversion, fragmentation, arpeggiation, scalar connection, quotation and sustain. These are composable operations, not a stock “Govan” lick bank. Original interview.

Freedom with a harmonic reason

Allan Holdsworth describes composing harmonic directions that invite improvisation and keeping long solo takes to preserve a larger shape. Our inference is to evaluate connected foreground material against harmonic destinations while retaining its own identity. Wide intervals and chromatic motion need direction and breathing room; note density alone does not create an ethereal solo. 1996 interview.

In their retrospective on Altered State, Jay Postones and Amos Williams describe substantial restructuring and the importance of relationships between songs to the album’s complete journey. This supports evaluating large-scale relationships as well as individual gestures. It does not establish an exact rhythmic recipe. The instrument’s persistent reference pulse and independently introduced riffs are our testable engineering response to the requested perceptual effect. Original Postones/Williams interview.

What the instrument now does

  • Several horizons: local voice-leading search, complete melodic arguments, independent rhythm epochs, and preparation/arrival/settling across several sections. Large expressive changes usually take time; declared cuts remain possible.
  • Different source shapes: ascending, descending, terraced, orbiting, pendulum and level identities. Dense realizations select related operations and rhythmic families by clause; every dense argument need not be the same arch at a different speed.
  • Explicit endings: a formal handoff can end open. Closed endings remain available where the larger plan actually settles. Open endings are not a failed tonic test.
  • Audible rhythmic reference: the regular pulse keeps its absolute phase through odd cells and meter changes. Riff and response layers have their own entrances and retirements. Shared accents still bind bass, accompaniment and drums.
  • Economical ornament: grace approaches, mordents, turns, trills, enclosures, passing chains, repetitions, anticipations, neighbor returns and connected slides borrow time inside eligible source notes. Protected heads, arrivals and written breaths survive. Not every passage should be decorated.
  • Native alternatives: 19-EDO is a frequent destination; 24- and 31-EDO add other native collections. Each mode generates candidates on its own grid with physical range and spacing constraints. The chosen 24-EDO wide/narrow thirds are colors, not a claim of classical tonal function. Matched additive roughness remains distinct from phrase syntax and expectation.

Six editable studies, one composer

Floating lines favors spacious native-19 phrases; Developing solo explores contrasting clause lengths and decoration; Interlocking drive combines denser arguments with a persistent pulse and overlapping cells; Travelling theme moves an established core through harmonic regions; Quarter-tone prism and Fine gravity expose the new pitch spaces. These are serializable parameter recipes in the same engine. They neither branch into artist-specific generators nor claim to reproduce those musicians.

The instrument’s Composition studies button keeps the seed and tempo for comparison. The audio laboratory renders complete first thematic arguments, both lead alone and full ensemble, and separately inspects the first four arguments for development. It reports actual onset gaps, directional changes, native-grid notes, ornaments, layers, replay and PCM measurements. This is intentionally not a one-variable experiment: each study specifies a different musical intention.

Evidence limit: the primary interviews were read; the recordings were not directly auditioned or fully transcribed here. Automated tests establish the ability to produce distinct relationships, preserve identities, and render correct sound. A listener must still judge whether the phrasing is compelling, whether the pulse is easy to feel, and whether the larger journey earns its contrasts.

01 / The compositional problem

Make the listener remember something before asking them to follow its transformation.

A stream of individually plausible notes is not yet a theme. A theme gives the listener a few relationships worth retaining: a characteristic opening, a particular pattern of attacks and silences, a contour with landmarks, and a sense of how its phrases continue or end. The useful engineering distinction is between identity-bearing decisions and realization decisions.

Identity survives

Choose a small set of protected relationships: head rhythm, characteristic interval, rest placement, stressed anchor, or question/answer pairing. Not every theme needs all of them. Record which ones this particular theme depends on.

Development explains change

A return can answer an earlier open ending, sequence an established cell toward an apex, reveal the same line over a new bass, or shorten a gesture to accelerate an arrival. Each operation needs a reason and a destination.

Working hypothesis: a better core is not necessarily slower, more diatonic, less syncopated, or more repetitive. It is more selective about what the listener is supposed to recognize. Beauty and memorability remain listening judgments; the machinery below makes those judgments easier to test.

Evidence labels used below: score observation means a licensed excerpt was visually inspected; reported analysis attributes a scholar’s or orchestra educator’s interpretation; design inference is our proposed generalization. Composer testimony supports stated working methods, not an inferred note-by-note recipe. No listening session or complete original-score analysis is claimed.

New / Density, register and orchestral range

Fast thought, spacious thought, and players who share an idea.

The v14 work addresses a different weakness from theme recognition: an identifiable head can still become monotonous if every occurrence has the same length, register, accompaniment and entrance. The new controls separate idea density, playing activity, ensemble size and intensity. A fast exposed line, a slow large ensemble and a tightly repeating band riff should all be possible.

Evidence limit: the sources below were read, and the published Dream Theater score sample was visually inspected. No direct listening session or complete transcription of these recordings is claimed. Track-specific descriptions are attributed to their speakers; procedural rules are our design inferences. The instrument does not contain copied riffs or artist presets.

Jordan Rudess · Insectsamongus and keyboard composition

The official release places Insectsamongus on Rhythm of Time (2004). Its metadata establishes provenance, not an analysis of the piece. Rudess’s own conservatory syllabus teaches accent shifts on repeated patterns, displacement, mirroring and contrasting groupings in 5/8 and 7/8. His KVR interview describes timed arpeggios through several octaves and back, octave switching and expressive pitch control. In a MusicPlayers interview he distinguishes slow melodic work from virtuosity according to the piece, and discusses building passages in layers. His SonicAbuse interview describes breathing space and a contrasting timbral variation of an existing riff.

Design inference: density should change the authored argument, not merely add ornaments. Preserve a subject’s contour and grouping while diminishing it, sequencing it toward a registral apex, answering it, then returning or landing. Instrument changes can reinterpret the same subject. This is a practical hypothesis inspired by those methods, not a claim to reproduce Insectsamongus.

Periphery · listen to the riff before choosing the beat

In Modern Drummer’s June 2019 interview, pp. 8–9, Matt Halpern describes learning guitar parts and melodies, then finding and expanding fitting rhythms. The Halpern/Nolly recording interview emphasizes precise ensemble accents and leaving space around a slow, powerful riff instead of automatically adding busier drums.

Design inference: give bass, kick and accompaniment access to one reusable onset/rest cell. Snare and cymbals can clarify its meter or reinterpret its feel. Keep a recognizable statement and change its answer or ending, rather than sampling an unrelated groove every bar.

Darkane · Layers of Lies

In the 2005 album interview, Peter Wildoer describes an intentional mix of technical playing and accessible choruses, clearer fast guitar detail, and a more direct rock-band arrangement with fewer keyboard layers. He distinguishes the title track’s heavier riffing from the speed and melodic chorus of Secondary Effects, and favors predominantly fast material with selected slower contrasts. Separately, Modern Drummer’s interview records his move toward more selective, sometimes laid-back playing while retaining demanding passages; its writer identifies particular instrumental and sixteenth-note examples.

Design inference: an opening need not earn permission to be fast through a slow introduction. Alternate sustained drive, sudden attacks, sparse exposure and dense development. Density is selective: the listener must still be able to distinguish the riff, the answer and the arrival.

Dream Theater · forward motion without constant BPM changes

The licensed Hudson Music sample includes Mike Portnoy’s Pull Me Under commentary (printed p. 11), Steve Ferraro’s transcription notes (p. 12), and the opening notation (p. 13). Portnoy describes successive half-time, backbeat, downbeat and upbeat interpretations. Ferraro identifies a constant quarter-note tempo of 102 despite the changing apparent speed, beat displacement, and different ride interpretations over recurring double-bass material. The sample supports those attributed observations; it is not a complete album analysis.

Design inference: retain the transport tempo while changing subdivision and half/full/double-time feel. A recurring riff can gain urgency because of which layer reveals the pulse. Returning to the stable interpretation is a meaningful resolution.

Porcupine Tree · Fear of a Blank Planet

In the 2007 Gavin Harrison interview, he explains how parts were developed for the pieces and refined through live performance before final recording. His account of Anesthetize starts with Steven Wilson’s framework and proceeds through individual and ensemble revisions. He also distinguishes rhythmic illusion and emotional effect from speed alone. His separately discussed instructional tempo relationships are not evidence of a particular ratio throughout this album.

Design inference: coherent material can persist while its orchestration, placement and perceived pulse change. A drum pattern needs a phrase-level job and a relationship to the band, including permission to leave space.

Koji Kondo · original Super Mario Bros. Ground Theme

Rhythmic specificity can be the stable core.

Score observation. The Nintendo-supervised piano preview distinguishes a short opening from repeated A/B material. Accents, rests, offbeat attacks, leaps, and chromatic approaches are explicit. Phrase shapes recur; activity is not uniformly dense. The arrangement retains tonal punctuation without making every small unit a final stop. Its simplified texture is not evidence for the precise NES voicing. Licensed preview, printed p. 4.

Composer evidence. Kondo explains that his first version matched the scenery but did not fit running and jumping; playing the prototype led to the replacement. This supports rhythm as a compositional premise, not a cosmetic layer. Nintendo interview.

Design inference. Store syncopated onset/rest topology as an invariant. Vary the continuation or bass while preserving the head’s placement against the pulse. A “familiarity” control should increase recognizable rhythmic returns, not quantize everything onto strong beats.

Scope: the original Ground Theme, not the many later Mario themes. The preview does not establish the complete original loop order.

Koji Kondo · original The Legend of Zelda Main / Overworld Theme

A destination makes the flourish meaningful.

Score observation. The licensed opening separates a triplet-driven introduction from the main melodic entrance. Longer anchors alternate with fast approaches, an upward trajectory, and descending replies. The bass changes beneath related melodic shapes; the excerpt is not simply a static major-triad fanfare. Nintendo-supervised preview, printed p. 6.

Analytical reading. Held anchors and directed motion create hierarchy: some notes arrive, others connect. The visible page does not establish the complete loop’s terminal cadence; that requires the remaining edition.

Design inference. Plan an arrival before filling its approach. Preserve a recognizable launch, allow one sequence to enlarge the register, then answer it. A leap must have a rhetorical role; repeatedly moving the same flourish upward is not sufficient development.

Scope: original Main Theme, not Zelda’s Lullaby or the separate Title Theme. Edition metadata.

John Williams · The Imperial March

Economy gives the chromatic continuation something to disturb.

Score observation. Repeated measured attacks and a dotted tail establish the opening. Later material introduces wider register and chromatic movement while recalling that rhythmic vocabulary. The licensed simplified arrangement visibly changes harmony beneath the line; its exact accompaniment must not be mistaken for Williams’s full orchestration. Carol Matz arrangement, printed p. 14.

Research context. Arthur, Lehman, and McNamara treat Star Wars themes as recurring identities, with separate encodings for melody, harmony, pedals, cadence, and boundary type. Their reductions deliberately choose representative statements, not every film variation. The Imperial March appears as an encoding example. Star Wars Thematic Corpus paper.

Design inference. Fix the attack vocabulary, vary phrase destination and harmonic context. Repeated notes can carry identity rather than signal failure. Distinguish a head, a contrasting continuation, and a return; do not equate “menacing” with a dissonance scalar.

The full concert march and short dramatic film statements have different formal spans. No complete concert-form label is asserted from this excerpt.

John Williams · Hedwig’s Theme

A familiar shape can contain a precise unfamiliar turn.

Score observation. The licensed orchestral arrangement opens in triple meter with a pickup, a long arrival, a dotted rhythmic cell, and conspicuous chromatic inflections. The melodic rhythm returns before its continuation changes. These are organized surprises within a recognizable shape. The first page alone cannot verify the full theme’s cadence or later formal sections. Michael Story arrangement, printed p. 2.

Composer evidence. In his separate Children’s Suite notes, Williams describes choosing instruments to suit individual identities and associates Hedwig’s Flight with celesta and later violins. This supports timbre as part of characterization; it does not establish that timbre replaces melodic construction. Williams’s program note.

Design inference. A signature chromatic note may be protected, not “corrected” to a new nearest scale tone on each frame. Keep its preparation and departure meaningful. Let a distinctive color return at a structural moment rather than randomizing every note’s instrument.

Joe Hisaishi · Howl’s Moving Castle

A stable dance can support a large emotional argument.

Reported analysis. The Milwaukee Symphony’s teaching guide describes a persistent waltz rhythm with major/minor contrast and models its selected rendition as AABA. That is a useful large-scale listening map, not proof that every film cue or concert arrangement shares one bar count. Program note, p. 18; form lesson, p. 24.

Design reading to verify by listening. Separate the recurring melody from the dance motor. Examine how a returning contour is carried through changed color, how continuation leads toward a high point, and where a phrase sounds open versus settled. Those are the useful cadence questions; this study has not inspected a complete licensed score and therefore does not assign exact cadential chords or measure numbers.

Design inference. Fix a dance identity across several phrases; vary the answer’s contour, harmonic region, and orchestral breadth. A contrasting middle needs a return path. A repeated accompaniment can provide continuity while the foreground changes.

Edition caution: Hisaishi’s 2023 symphonic recording is a newly arranged concert version. The composer-approved Zen-On piano collection is a separate reference edition; its unavailable preview was not treated as inspected notation.

Joe Hisaishi · One Summer’s Day / Spirited Away

Harmonic ambiguity need not dissolve melodic identity.

Reported analysis. Sungwijit’s study follows a 93-measure extended piano realization: introduction, two themes and bridge, then transformed returns and ending. It identifies fourth-related sonorities, descending bass, sequence and repetition, followed by fragment transposition, augmentation/diminution, and changing texture. The ending slows the melody over suspended colors rather than being reduced to a textbook authentic-cadence formula. Sungwijit, 2021, pp. 477–485.

Scope and contour. The paper’s diagrams support changing register and duration around related material; they do not justify imposing one invariant arch on all versions. Its cited piano edition is associated with Encore. Treat this as analysis of that extended realization, not a timing map of every soundtrack appearance.

Design inference. Preserve a singable phrase while upper structures and bass reinterpret it. A delayed answer can be clarified through register, duration, and bass arrival even when the chord field remains ambiguous. Later elaboration should point back to identified fragments rather than merely add faster notes.

Shared lesson, not shared style: choose what must remain recognizable, make continuity audible, and reserve change for a meaningful contrast. The particular invariant can be syncopation, a repeated attack, a pickup, an interval, a dance pulse, or a relationship between two phrases.

Function, Tonnetz relations, and third cycles are different tools.

Cohn’s account makes neo-Riemannian relations useful for chromatic triadic passages that resist a single diatonic key. In conventional 12-tone P/L/R operations, two pitch classes remain common while the third changes; the familiar Tonnetz places thirds and fifths on different axes. Such a map models relations, not a complete composition. Cohn, 1998, especially pp. 169–175.

A hexatonic cycle is a specific alternating transformational construction, not a synonym for any sequence of third-related roots. Berry and Fiore formalize its shared-tone and minimal-motion properties. Berry & Fiore, Hexatonic Systems. Separately, a chord-space model can compare realized voice-leading paths; Tymoczko’s geometry concerns that kind of relationship. Tymoczko, 2006.

Coltrane changes: distant centers, locally directed arrivals

Reported analysis. In Giant Steps, John Coltrane organizes three major-third-related centers—B, G and E-flat—and connects local tonic arrivals through dominant or ii–V preparation. McClimon distinguishes that directed local function from harmony that merely floats between unrelated colors. His analysis also discusses Coltrane changes as a substitution that delays an original ii–V–I destination through intervening third-related centers. McClimon, 2016, pp. 81–83.

Keller’s teaching note emphasizes the speed and placement of changes, not a large number of keys: the 16-bar tune gives different spans to its local preparations. Thus a “Coltrane variation” should specify arrival timing as well as regional order. Keller, Remembering “Giant Steps” Changes.

Design inference. Keep an original theme’s anchors; choose third-related regions and give their dominants real resolution obligations. Vary regional direction, dwell time and preparation density within the phrase’s arrival contract. Tonnetz operations describe local common-tone relations; Coltrane-style regional planning supplies a different layer of tonal direction. Smooth voicing can realize either, but equivalent voice-leading geometry does not make them the same procedure.

Current tool: 12-TET regions 0 / 4 / 8 form equal thirds. Native 19 regions 0 / 6 / 12 close with unequal 6 / 6 / 7 steps. That is an explicit adaptation, not Coltrane’s tuning or a transcription of his changes.

Proposed architecture. Phrase intent chooses a destination and deadline; a harmonic field supplies possible regions; a local relation graph proposes bridges; the voice planner chooses actual pitches. Keep separate scores for cadenceFit, regionalNovelty, commonTones and registeredMotion. A low-motion candidate may still be wrong because it postpones an arrival indefinitely.

Closure also depends on placement and presentation. An experiment with Mozart sonata excerpts found that cadential category, formal context, melodic dissonance and rhetorical features affect perceived closure. This is evidence against treating a last chord alone as a sufficient cadence model; it does not prove one universal cadence grammar for game, film or microtonal music. Sears, Caplin & McAdams, 2014.

04 / Tuning and timbre

Carry relationships into native 19; do not pretend the 12-tone geometry is unchanged.

These six references belong to conventional Western notated traditions. They do not validate 19-EDO cadence perception or a native-19 version of their thematic behavior. Transfer structural ideas—recognition, directed contour, stable anchors, contrasting endings—then test their tuned realization.

Concrete arithmetic, calculated here

One 19-EDO step is 1200 / 19 ≈ 63.158 cents. Using native triads [0, 6, 11] and [0, 5, 11], their thirds are approximately 378.947 and 315.789 cents, and their fifth is 694.737 cents. This is a specified working vocabulary, not an assertion that listeners hear every role identically to 12-TET.

For those triads, native analogues of P, L and R retain two tones but move the remaining voice by 1, 2 and 3 grid steps respectively. Their costs are therefore unequal in a different way from 12-TET. Repeatedly adding a native major third of 6 steps traverses all 19 pitch classes before closing: 19 / gcd(19, 6) = 19. A 12-TET major-third root cycle closes after three. Reusing that three-root loop in native 19 would silently introduce a discontinuity or a different interval.

Implementation contract. Store a structural degree or pitch-role relationship independently of its physical realization. Generate native grid pitches directly; preserve spelled/role distinctions where the model needs them. Validate common tones, actual cents moved, voice order, register and expected arrival after projection. Do not regenerate a melody on an intermediate MIDI grid and call the result native because the final oscillator is detuned.

Sensory roughness is a separate axis.

Sethares relates local sensory dissonance to interactions among spectral partials; changing spectrum can change which intervals are locally smoother. That describes an acoustic mechanism, not thematic identity or learned harmonic function. Sethares, Relating Tuning and Timbre. A quiet sustained tone can carry unresolved expectation; a rough chord can function as an arrival.

Use separate controls and diagnostics: spectral roughness, tonal ambiguity, cadential pressure, metric tension and textural intensity. In matched additive experiments, analysis and synthesis must use the same actual frequencies and partial amplitudes. In an ensemble synthesizer with changing timbres, a five-partial model is an approximation and should be labeled as such.

05 / Current application boundary

Implemented tools are a bounded first test of this strategy.

The current thematic path implements three original core grammars: period, sentence, and arch-reprise. Structural notes carry stable identity, degree, rhythm weight, role and purpose. Occurrences support development, reharmonization and sequence. These are deliberately small vocabularies, not a model of the six reference composers.

The harmonic route planner evaluates 32 complete destination alternatives against the authored melody, weighting each note’s exact overlap with the harmonic window and its structural role rather than treating distinct pitch classes equally. It then realizes complete chord tones with a root bass before local voicing evaluation. Its bounded vocabulary includes functional preparation, secondary-dominant resolution obligations, typed P/R/L operations on actual major/minor triads, and authentic, half, plagal or deceptive ending plans. A phrase with only one harmonic window reports no cadence because no preparation occurred. A named ending is an implemented constraint, not proof of a listener’s perceived cadence.

Native 19 uses explicit interval sets and a three-region 0 → 6 → 12 → 19 route. Its steps are 6 / 6 / 7, so this particular third-region route closes intentionally rather than claiming equal thirds. The arithmetic in the previous section explains why that distinction matters.

Still proposals: a larger family of rhythmic constitutions including strongly syncopated cores; generalized typed operator preconditions; listener-calibrated identity distance; broader cadence grammars; and adaptive long-term novelty budgets. The next section describes those extensions as design targets. Existing source and PCM checks establish concrete behavior, not artistic equivalence to the references.

06 / A practical deterministic composition strategy

Generate the argument first, then realize its surface.

The following is an original engineering proposal, not a reconstruction of any named composer’s process. It keeps the voice-based harmonic engine, but gives it longer intentions than the next plausible frame.

1. CoreIdentity and constraints
2. ArgumentQuestion, continuation, return
3. LandmarksAnchors and endings
4. RealizationVoices, rhythm, texture
5. MemoryWhat was actually heard

1. Give each seed a compact thematic constitution.

Sample a small number of complete candidates, then compare them as phrases. A useful initial search unit is an entire call-and-answer or presentation-and-continuation, not an isolated note. Establish a rhythmic word, one distinctive contour feature, two or three anchors, a comfortable tessitura, an opening identity, and an ending relationship. Some candidates may be lyrical, some syncopated, some declamatory; those are generic musical behaviors.

ThemeCore {
  id, version, originAddress
  head: { rhythmTicks, rests, contour, characteristicIntervals }
  anchors: [{ relativeTick, pitchRole, stress, structuralRole }]
  phraseRelations: [{ source, response, sharedHead, endingIntent }]
  register: { comfortableBand, apexBudget, exceptionalLeapBudget }
  invariants: IdentityConstraint[]
  allowedOperators: DevelopmentOperator[]
}

Keep an undeformed source. A “memory” containing only already ornamented MIDI events cannot distinguish the idea from its performance. Separately record heard coverage: a theme is established when its identifying material has actually sounded, not when a planner merely allocated it.

2. Build different causal arguments, not one obligatory arch.

Choose among a bounded set of relationships: question → answer; statement → sequence → return; interrupted statement → recovery → completion; stable refrain alternating with contrasting episodes. Phrase lengths can differ, but length should follow the argument. Long sections need additional thought, not time-stretching the same twenty notes.

Declare each ending as open, locally settled, redirected, interrupted, or final. Attach an intended bass/harmonic role and an arrival window. Plan a stronger closure after one or more open thoughts when the form calls for it. A loop boundary may invite continuation rather than imitate a final concert cadence.

The first return should usually preserve more than later development. Do not randomize rhythm, contour, mode, orchestration and register simultaneously, then award a “recall” bonus because a source ID matches. Track which relationships remain. Literal recurrence can be effective after enough intervening material; recent repetition and distant recognition should receive different treatment.

4. Plan melody and harmony together at structural notes.

Do not snap an authored long melody to each newly generated chord. Schedule structural anchors and their harmonic interpretations together. Between them, let the harmonic voices move smoothly while the melody uses steps, suspensions, anticipations or planned chromatic approaches. Long held melody notes can make a changing bass especially meaningful; they are not failures to maximize event density.

Use receding-horizon search inside these contracts. Candidate evaluation should include endpoint feasibility: can this locally appealing voicing still reach the planned arrival with bounded movement? Commit a short span, retaining the phrase’s identity and destination. When live controls make a contract impossible, choose a named adaptation—extend, redirect, interrupt—rather than silently losing the argument.

Weights are auditionable hypotheses. A positive “novelty” term must not reward any difference indiscriminately. Prefer an ordinary local event that makes a convincing later answer possible over a striking event that prevents the sentence from completing.

5. Let accompaniment support the sentence.

Assign rhythmic roles: a persistent pulse, sustained harmonic support, a bass trajectory, sparse structural punctuation, and a foreground. They need not attack together constantly. Shared accents at a head, apex or arrival can clarify a large phrase while independent movement continues between them.

Calm behavior needs slower harmonic gestures and audible sustain, not merely smaller velocities on plucks. Energetic behavior needs faster subdivision, deliberate articulation, register and coordinated preparation, not a global gain increase. A retreat should remove activity and release pressure over time. Keep theme-space and genuine ensemble silence distinct so a foreground breath does not automatically stop the entire world.

6. Make seed variation change a constitution, not every decision.

Address randomness by stable domain and musical identity: (seed, themeId, occurrenceId, operatorId, decisionId). Use separate domains for core selection, phrase relation, harmonic realization, groove and orchestration. A changed drum implementation should leave the core intact; adding an ornament must not shift every subsequent random choice.

Within a performance, retain the constitution long enough to be learned. Across seeds, vary rhythmic word, interval signature, phrase relation, register behavior and harmonic trajectory. Serialize the core, version, operators, automation and state needed for replay. Log actual realized events as the authority for exports and bookmarks.

Original structural example — not a quotation

One invented 12-bar plan: a three-bar call establishes a short pickup and one held anchor; a four-bar reply repeats the pickup, changes the tail, and ends open; two bars compress a source fragment toward a new register; three bars restore the opening rhythm and complete the promised arrival. A later return could preserve all anchor timings while changing the bass region.

These are original role and duration choices, not a transcription of any reference. The same logic can generate a syncopated theme, a restrained song, or an angular theme by choosing different constitutions.

07 / Tests that can change our minds

Separate correctness, perceptual evidence, and taste.

Symbolic checks

  • Protected head rhythm, anchors and rests survive specified returns.
  • Ornament reduction recovers the source core.
  • Open thoughts lead to an actual planned closure when required.
  • Sequence has direction and a bounded repetition count.
  • Native pitch, voice order and deterministic event hashes hold.
  • A new seed changes identity; a new drum pattern does not.

Rendered checks

  • Play the complete first argument through its closed ending.
  • Render lead alone, backing alone and full mix.
  • Inspect long-note sustain, intended gaps and audible foreground balance.
  • Measure finite samples, headroom, scheduling deadlines and actual tuned frequencies.
  • Measure the declared pitches and envelopes, not just metadata labels.

Listening protocol. Compare the same core in four conditions: plain statement, identity-preserving variation, deliberately scrambled control, and full arrangement. Ask listeners to identify the return, tap its rhythmic identity, indicate an expected ending, and hum or describe the head after intervening music. Ask separately about beauty, fatigue, surprise and desire to continue. Keep those responses separate from signal measurements.

Ablations. Remove orchestration; replace the harmonic bed with a neutral support; disable variation; preserve pitches but shuffle rhythm; preserve rhythm but scramble anchors. Each comparison tests a specific hypothesis. If only the lush patch makes a weak phrase tolerable, revise the core. If a rhythmic shuffle destroys recognition while pitch changes do not, give that theme a stronger rhythmic invariant.

Do not optimize one proxy. A high recurrence count can be irritating. A wide pitch range can sound aimless. A low roughness score can be dull. A high RMS contrast can bury the quiet statement. A large number of notes is not virtuosity. These metrics describe behavior; listeners decide whether that behavior communicates.

Recommended implementation order: complete unornamented cores and ending contracts → exact first returns → one-operator variations → joint anchor/harmony planning → larger contrast and memory → ornament and orchestral elaboration. At each step, keep an audible baseline and remove rules that fail the comparison.

    Institutional teaching note by its author; key-center and timing analysis. No complete progression is reproduced here.

    Author’s institutional publication record and abstract; supports geometric voice-leading as a distinct modeling question.

  17. David Sears, William E. Caplin & Stephen McAdams, Perceiving the Classical Cadence, Music Perception 31(5), 397–417 (2014).

    Primary perceptual experiment in a particular tonal repertoire. Supports contextual closure distinctions, not universal emotion or native-19 claims.

  18. William Sethares, Relating Tuning and Timbre.

    Author’s explanation and demonstrations of sensory dissonance versus spectrum and tuning. Useful acoustic model; separate from phrase syntax and aesthetic value.

Continuum research reference. Analysis supports testable compositional hypotheses; it does not certify that generated music is beautiful or that listeners will recognize every intended relationship. The next decisive evidence is a complete, audible original theme and a convincing return.
